How Long Does Grief Last After Losing a Pet?

6 min read · August 2026

If you're asking this question, chances are you're somewhere in the middle of it — far enough from the day it happened that you thought you'd feel steadier by now, but still catching yourself crying in the car, or reaching for a leash that isn't there.

So let's answer it honestly, because you deserve honesty more than comfort: there is no fixed timeline for grieving a pet. And the fact that you still hurt does not mean something is wrong with you.

Why there's no set answer

Grief isn't a wound that heals on a schedule. It's the natural shape love takes when the thing you loved is gone. The depth of your grief tends to match the depth of the bond — not the size of the animal, not how long you had them, not what anyone else thinks is "reasonable."

Some people feel lighter within a few weeks. Others carry it for months, or feel it resurface a year later on a birthday or an anniversary. Studies on pet bereavement have found that acute grief commonly lasts anywhere from one to several months, with softer waves continuing well beyond that. All of it is normal.

The first weeks are the heaviest

In the beginning, grief is loud. It's in the empty food bowl, the quiet at the door, the habits your body still performs before your mind catches up. This raw stage is exhausting, and it's supposed to be. You're not failing at coping — you're in the hardest part.

Be gentle with yourself here. Sleep when you can. Eat something. Let the small tasks be enough. You don't have to be productive through this.

Grief comes in waves, not stages

You may have heard grief described as neat "stages." In reality, it moves in waves. You'll have a good morning and a shattered afternoon. You'll go a whole week feeling okay, then a song or a smell will knock you flat. This isn't backsliding. It's just how grief works.

"Grief doesn't get smaller. You grow around it — and slowly, there's more of you than there is of the ache."

It changes — it doesn't end

Here's the part that surprises people: grief for a beloved pet rarely disappears completely, and it doesn't need to. What changes is its texture. The sharp, breathtaking pain of the first weeks softens over time into something quieter — a tenderness that visits when you think of them, more warm than unbearable.

One day you'll notice that a memory made you smile before it made you cry. That's not forgetting. That's healing. Missing them is simply the tax you pay on having loved them so well.

When grief might need extra support

Grief is not a problem to be fixed. But sometimes it becomes something heavier that deserves help. Consider reaching out to a pet-loss support group or a grief counselor if, after a couple of months, you notice that you:

— Can't function in your daily life, work, or relationships
— Feel stuck in intense guilt or self-blame
— Are withdrawing completely from people and things you once cared about
— Feel hopeless in a way that frightens you

Pet loss is a recognized area of grief counseling. Asking for support isn't weakness — it's the same care you'd have given them.

Let the memories stay close

Many people try to speed up healing by putting everything away — the photos, the collar, the toys. It feels safer not to look. But grief researchers consistently find the opposite helps: gently keeping a connection to the one you lost, rather than severing it, is what lets grief settle over time.

You don't have to do it now. But when you're ready, having a place to keep the photos, the stories, and the little things only you would remember can turn grief from something you flinch away from into something you can hold.
