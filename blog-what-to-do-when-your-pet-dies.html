What to Do When Your Pet Dies: A Gentle First-Steps Guide

6 min read · August 2026

If you're reading this in the raw first hours after losing your pet, we're so sorry. This is a calm, practical guide for what to do next — written to take a little weight off your shoulders at a moment when it's hard to think clearly. Take it one step at a time. There's no rush.

First, breathe. You have time.

Whatever you decide, you don't have to decide it this second. It's okay to sit with your pet for a while, to say goodbye, to let other family members see them if they need to. These quiet moments matter, and you won't get them back — so if you need them, take them.

Caring for their body in the meantime

If your pet died at home and you need a little time before deciding on aftercare, you can keep them cool and comfortable in the meantime. Gently place them on a towel or blanket, curled as if sleeping, in a cool room. If it will be more than a few hours, a garage or a cool space helps. It's a tender thing to do, and doing it gently can be part of the goodbye.

Call your veterinarian

Your vet's office is the best first call. They can guide you through your options, arrange cremation, and often store your pet until arrangements are made. If your pet passed unexpectedly and you want to understand why, ask about your options while you're on the phone. Vet teams handle this every day with great compassion — let them help carry some of it.

Choosing aftercare: cremation or burial

There's no "right" choice here — only the one that feels right for you. The common options are:

— Private cremation: Your pet is cremated alone and their ashes are returned to you, often in an urn. This is the most popular choice for those who want to keep something tangible.
— Communal cremation: A lower-cost option where ashes are not returned.
— Home burial: Allowed in many areas if you own your property — check your local regulations first, and bury deep enough (usually at least 3 feet) in a spot that feels meaningful.
— Pet cemetery: A dedicated resting place you can visit, with a marker or headstone.

Local laws on home burial and body handling vary by region — a quick check with your vet or municipality is worth it.

Tell the people who need to know

When you're ready, let close family and friends know — especially children, and anyone who loved your pet too. You may also want to notify your vet to close their records, and cancel any upcoming appointments, grooming, or food deliveries. These small logistics can sting to handle, so ask someone to help if you can.

"In the first hours, you're not making perfect decisions. You're just loving them through one more thing. That's enough."

Give yourself permission to grieve

Once the practical steps are handled, the quiet sets in — and that's when grief often arrives in full. Don't be surprised by how hard it hits. Losing a pet is a genuine loss, and you're allowed to fall apart, to take a day off, to cry as much as you need. You're not overreacting. You loved someone, and they're gone.

When you're ready, gather what you have of them

In the days ahead, you might find yourself scrolling through photos, half-smiling, half-breaking. That instinct to hold onto them is worth following. Before the little details start to blur — their favorite spot, the sound they made at the door, the way they greeted you — it can help to gather everything in one place: the photos, the stories, the number of days you had together.

Not to move on from them. To keep them close.
